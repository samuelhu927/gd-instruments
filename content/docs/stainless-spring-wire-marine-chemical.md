---
title: "Stainless Steel Spring Wire in Marine and Chemical Environments: 316 vs 302 vs 17-7PH"
date: 2020-11-03
layout: "single"
---
Stainless steel springs are often specified for marine and chemical environments on the assumption that “stainless” means “corrosion-proof.” In reality, the corrosion resistance of stainless spring wire varies significantly with grade, and the selection among 302, 316, and 17-7PH involves trade-offs between corrosion resistance, strength, and cost that must be matched to the specific environment.

302 stainless steel is a standard austenitic grade with approximately 18% chromium and 8% nickel. It provides good resistance to atmospheric corrosion and to many mild chemical environments, but it is susceptible to pitting and crevice corrosion in chloride-containing environments—including seawater and salt spray. The chloride ion attacks the passive oxide layer, and once a pit initiates, it can propagate into a crack under the combined action of corrosion and stress. For marine environments, 302 is generally not recommended for critical springs unless the spring is protected by a coating or is operating in a sheltered location with limited chloride exposure.

316 stainless steel contains approximately 2–3% molybdenum in addition to the chromium and nickel. The molybdenum significantly improves resistance to pitting and crevice corrosion in chloride environments, making 316 the standard choice for marine and chemical applications where 302 would be marginal. 316 is not immune to corrosion—it can still pit in concentrated chlorides or in stagnant seawater—but its performance is substantially better than 302 in the same environment. The trade-off is cost: 316 wire is more expensive than 302, and its cold-drawn strength may be slightly lower for the same drawing reduction, because the alloying additions affect the work-hardening behavior.

17-7PH is a precipitation-hardening stainless steel that achieves higher strength than 302 or 316 through an aging treatment. Its corrosion resistance is similar to 302, not 316, because it does not contain molybdenum. This is a critical distinction: 17-7PH is often selected for its strength and its ability to retain mechanical properties at elevated temperatures (up to approximately 600°F or 316°C), but it is not the grade to choose for chloride-rich environments simply because it is “stainless.” A 17-7PH spring in a marine environment may corrode at a rate comparable to a 302 spring, and the higher strength of 17-7PH makes it more susceptible to stress-corrosion cracking once a pit has initiated.

The selection logic for marine and chemical environments is therefore: use 316 when chloride resistance is the primary requirement and the strength level can be achieved with cold-drawn austenitic wire; use 302 only for mild environments or where the spring is protected; use 17-7PH when elevated-temperature strength retention is required and the environment is not chloride-rich. For extremely corrosive environments—concentrated acids, high-chloride brines, or high-temperature seawater—nickel alloys such as Inconel 718 or Hastelloy C-276 may be necessary, because even 316 has limits in these aggressive conditions. The grade designation alone is not sufficient; the specific environment and the stress level must be considered together.
