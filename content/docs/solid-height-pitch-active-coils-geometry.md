---
title: "Solid Height, Pitch and Active Coils: The Geometry That Defines a Compression Spring"
date: 2020-03-02
layout: "single"
---
The geometry of a compression spring is defined by a small set of parameters that are coupled: wire diameter, mean coil diameter, active coils, pitch, free length, and solid height. These parameters cannot be specified independently; changing one changes the others, and the consequences propagate into stiffness, stress, and manufacturability.

Solid height is the axial length of the spring when all coils are in contact, often called the compressed height or the solid length. For an unground spring, the solid height is approximately the product of the number of coils and the wire diameter. For a ground and squared spring, the solid height is reduced because the end coils are ground flat, and the effective number of coils contributing to the solid stack is reduced by the amount of grinding. The solid height is not merely a dimensional reference; it defines the physical limit of compression and therefore the maximum stress that the spring can experience without coil-to-coil contact. A spring that is compressed to solid height develops a stress that is significantly higher than its normal working stress, and if that solid-height stress exceeds the material’s yield strength, the spring will take a permanent set.

Pitch is the axial distance between corresponding points on adjacent active coils, measured when the spring is in the free (unloaded) condition. Pitch and free length are related through the number of active coils: the free length is approximately the product of pitch and active coils plus the contribution of the end coils. Pitch must be large enough to prevent coil contact at the maximum working deflection, but not so large that the spring becomes unstable or that the helix angle introduces significant bending stress in addition to torsion. A common design error is to specify a pitch that provides adequate clearance at the nominal working load but ignores tolerance stack-up: wire diameter tolerance, coil diameter tolerance, and heat-treatment distortion can reduce the actual clearance, leading to premature coil contact and a stiffness curve that rises sharply before the design maximum load.

Active coils are the coils that contribute to the spring’s deflection under load. The total number of coils includes the active coils plus the end coils, which may be closed and ground, closed and not ground, or open. The distinction matters because the stiffness formula for a compression spring, K = Gd⁴ / (8D³n), uses the number of active coils, not the total number of coils. If a spring is specified with a total of 10 coils but the end coils are closed and ground such that only 7.5 coils are active, using 10 in the stiffness calculation will significantly underestimate the spring rate. The active coil count is determined by the end configuration and the grinding practice, and it must be confirmed by measurement or by the spring manufacturer’s process specification.

The practical implication of this coupled geometry is that a compression spring drawing is not complete unless it specifies wire diameter with tolerance, mean coil diameter with tolerance, free length with tolerance, total coils, and end configuration. A drawing that specifies only free length, diameter, and “spring rate” leaves the manufacturer to choose the combination of active coils, pitch, and end treatment, and different choices can produce springs that meet the rate specification but differ in solid height, stress at working load, and fatigue life. The geometry of a compression spring is a system, and the parameters must be specified and verified as a system.
