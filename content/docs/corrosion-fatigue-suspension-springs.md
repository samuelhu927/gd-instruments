---
title: "Corrosion Fatigue of Suspension Springs: When Salt Spray and Cyclic Load Act Together"
date: 2019-08-27
layout: "single"
---
Suspension springs in automotive and off-highway applications are exposed to a combination of cyclic mechanical loading and corrosive environments—road salt, humidity, and splashed electrolyte solutions. The interaction between corrosion and fatigue is not simply additive; corrosion pits act as stress concentrators and hydrogen sources, and the fatigue life of a corroded spring can be an order of magnitude lower than the fatigue life of the same spring tested in air.

The mechanism of corrosion fatigue in spring steel involves the initiation of pits at surface discontinuities—inclusions, grain boundaries, or mechanical damage—followed by crack initiation from the pit. The pit concentrates the applied stress and provides a local environment where hydrogen can be generated by the corrosion reaction and absorbed into the steel. The crack then propagates under cyclic loading, with the corrosion process continuing to modify the crack tip chemistry and the crack growth rate. The result is a fracture that may exhibit both fatigue features (striations) and corrosion features (corrosion products on the fracture surface, intergranular facets near the initiation site).

A standard test for evaluating corrosion fatigue resistance is cyclic salt spray exposure combined with mechanical loading. One commonly used cycle is: salt water spray (5% NaCl) at 35°C and 95% relative humidity for 3 hours, followed by natural drying at 35°C and 65% relative humidity for 21 hours. This 24-hour cycle is repeated for a specified number of cycles, and the spring is examined for corrosion pits and cracks. The test can be conducted with or without simultaneous mechanical loading; when loading is applied during the salt spray exposure, the test captures the interaction between corrosion and fatigue more directly.

The results of corrosion fatigue testing are used to select surface treatments and coating systems. A coating that provides good barrier protection in a static salt spray test may perform poorly under cyclic loading, because the coating can crack or spall at the high-stress regions of the spring and expose the steel to the electrolyte. For suspension springs, the coating system must be flexible enough to accommodate the deflection of the spring without cracking, and it must maintain adhesion under cyclic loading. Zinc phosphate coatings, epoxy powder coatings, and zinc-rich paints are common choices, but the performance depends on the substrate preparation and the coating thickness.

The design of the spring also affects corrosion fatigue resistance. Compressive residual stresses introduced by shot peening oppose crack initiation and can extend corrosion fatigue life, provided the peening is performed before the coating and the coating does not relax the compressive layer. The geometry of the spring—particularly the regions of highest stress and the areas where water can accumulate—should be considered in the design stage. A spring that has a sharp transition or a recess where salt water can pool will be more susceptible to corrosion fatigue than a spring with a smooth profile and drainage.
