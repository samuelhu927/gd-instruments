title: "Spring Failure Analysis: Reading the Fracture Surface from Beach Marks to Dimples"
date: 2020-05-12
layout: "single"
---
Spring failure analysis begins with the fracture surface. The topography of the fracture—its macroscopic features and microscopic morphology—encodes the loading history, the crack initiation site, and the failure mechanism. A systematic reading of the fracture surface can distinguish fatigue from overload, identify the role of corrosion or hydrogen, and point to the manufacturing or design variable that needs to be corrected.

The first distinction is between fatigue and non-fatigue failure. A fatigue fracture typically exhibits three regions: a crack initiation site, a stable crack propagation region, and a final overload region. The propagation region often shows beach marks—macroscopic curved lines that represent successive positions of the crack front—and, at higher magnification, fatigue striations, which are microscopic ridges corresponding to individual load cycles. The final overload region is rougher, because the remaining cross-section is too small to carry the load and the fracture occurs rapidly by ductile or brittle mechanisms. The presence of beach marks or striations is diagnostic of fatigue; their absence suggests that the failure was not driven by cyclic loading.

The crack initiation site provides the most direct clue to the root cause. If initiation occurred at a surface defect—a grinding mark, a corrosion pit, a decarburized layer—the manufacturing or service environment is implicated. If initiation occurred at a subsurface inclusion or a pore, the material cleanliness or the casting/rolling process is implicated. If initiation occurred at a change in section or a sharp corner, the design stress concentration is implicated. The location of the initiation site relative to the spring geometry—for example, on the inside of the coil versus the outside—can also indicate whether the failure was driven by torsion, bending, or a combination of loads.

Ductile fracture surfaces are characterized by dimples, which are microscopic voids that coalesced during the final fracture. Dimples indicate that the material underwent plastic deformation before fracture, which is typical of overload in a ductile material. Cleavage fracture surfaces are characterized by flat facets and river patterns, indicating brittle fracture with little plastic deformation. Intergranular fracture surfaces show the outlines of prior austenite grains, and they are characteristic of hydrogen embrittlement, stress-corrosion cracking, or temper embrittlement. The distinction between these morphologies is not always clear-cut; mixed-mode fractures can exhibit dimples in some regions and cleavage or intergranular facets in others.

The fracture surface must be protected and cleaned before examination. Rust, debris, and handling damage can obscure the features that are diagnostic. The surface should be photographed in the as-received condition, then cleaned with a solvent or a mild chemical treatment that removes corrosion products without attacking the steel. For detailed examination, scanning electron microscopy is used to resolve striations, dimples, and intergranular facets at high magnification. The combination of macroscopic photography and microscopic examination provides the complete picture that is needed to move from “where did it break” to “why did it break.”
