---
title: "High-Cycle Fatigue of Spring Steel Wire: Why 10⁶ Cycles Is Not the End of the Story"
date: 2020-07-27
layout: "single"
---
High-cycle fatigue testing of spring steel wire is typically conducted to construct an S-N curve, and the conventional fatigue limit is often defined at 10⁶ or 10⁷ cycles. The choice of cycle count is not arbitrary, and the difference between 10⁶ and 10⁷ cycles can be the difference between a spring that passes a fatigue test and a spring that fails in service.

Research on high-strength spring steel wire using ultrasonic fatigue testing has shown that the stress-life (S-N) curve can become horizontal at a maximum stress of approximately 800 MPa after 10⁶ cycles. This horizontal region is often interpreted as the fatigue limit: below this stress, the material is assumed to have infinite life. However, other studies have shown that fatigue rupture can occur beyond 10⁷ cycles, and that the S-N curve corresponding to 10⁷ cycles can deviate significantly from the curve at 10⁶ cycles. The existence of a true fatigue limit—a stress below which no failure occurs regardless of cycle count—is still debated, and for high-strength spring steels, the safe assumption is that the S-N curve continues to decline slowly beyond 10⁷ cycles rather than becoming perfectly horizontal.

The practical implication for spring design is that a spring qualified at 10⁶ cycles is not automatically qualified for 10⁷ cycles at the same stress level. The S-N curve between these two cycle counts has a finite slope, and the stress that produces 10⁶ cycles of life may produce only a fraction of that life at 10⁷ cycles. For automotive suspension springs, valve springs, and other high-cycle applications, the required cycle count is often 10⁷ or higher, and the fatigue test must be conducted to the required cycle count rather than to a lower count with an assumption of infinite life.

The statistical treatment of fatigue data adds another layer of uncertainty. The S-N curves determined from a limited number of specimens have confidence intervals, and the curves corresponding to 10⁶ and 10⁷ cycles can deviate from the 95% confidence interval of the data. This means that a design based on the mean S-N curve may have an unacceptable probability of failure at the required cycle count. For critical springs, the design stress must be selected with a margin that accounts for the statistical scatter of the fatigue data, not just the mean curve.

The testing method also influences the result. Ultrasonic fatigue testing, which operates at approximately 20 kHz, allows high-cycle fatigue data to be generated in a reasonable time, but the loading conditions differ from conventional servo-hydraulic testing at lower frequencies. The frequency effect on fatigue life is material-dependent and can be significant for steels that exhibit strain-rate sensitivity or internal heating at high frequency. For spring wire, the conventional approach is to use servo-hydraulic or electromagnetic testing at frequencies that are high enough to be practical but low enough to avoid excessive heating and to remain representative of service conditions.
