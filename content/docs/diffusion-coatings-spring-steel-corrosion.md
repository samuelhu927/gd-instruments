---
title: "Diffusion Coatings for Spring Steel: Al and Ni-Al as Alternatives to Paint"
date: 2020-02-25
layout: "single"
---
Conventional barrier coatings—paints, platings, and polymer films—protect spring steel by isolating the surface from the corrosive environment. However, barrier coatings can fail by peeling, cracking, or spalling, especially when the spring deflects under load. Diffusion coatings offer an alternative approach: instead of applying a layer on top of the steel, the coating elements diffuse into the steel surface and form intermetallic compounds that are integral to the substrate.

A 1984 study on diffusion coatings for spring steel investigated aluminum and nickel-aluminum coatings applied by metal spraying followed by heating in the austenite region. After heating at austenitizing temperature for 10 minutes, a diffusion layer of approximately 50 μm was observed. The coated specimens were then quenched in oil and tempered at 450°C, followed by shot peening. The diffusion layers consisted of intermetallic compounds: Fe₂Al₅ (eta phase) for aluminum coating and Ni₃Al (gamma-prime phase) for nickel-aluminum alloy coating. The hardness of the aluminum diffusion layer was HV 550, and the nickel-aluminum layer was HV 600.

The corrosion performance of the diffusion-coated springs was evaluated by cyclic exposure between salt spray and air for 20 days. The specimens with diffusion layers exhibited no corrosion pits on the surface, while specimens without diffusion coating showed etch-pits approximately 0.13 mm deep. This result demonstrates that the diffusion coating provides a genuine corrosion-resistant surface that is not subject to the peeling failure mode of barrier coatings. The intermetallic layer is metallurgically bonded to the steel, so it cannot delaminate under mechanical stress or thermal cycling.

An important finding of the study was the interaction between the diffusion coating and shot peening. The thickness of the metal-sprayed layer before heating was responsible for the amount of residual stress on the surface after shot peening. When the sprayed layer thickness was restricted to within 50 μm, the layer was fully absorbed into the steel during heating, leaving no scale to interfere with shot peening. Under these conditions, the shot peening effect was maintained, and the specimens had a fatigue strength equivalent to that of uncoated springs. This is a critical point: a diffusion coating that is too thick can leave an unabsorbed layer that interferes with shot peening, and the loss of compressive residual stress can negate the corrosion benefit.

For spring manufacturers, diffusion coatings are not a drop-in replacement for paint or plating. The process requires a high-temperature treatment that may affect the spring’s heat treatment and dimensions, and the coating thickness must be carefully controlled to avoid interfering with subsequent shot peening. However, for applications where barrier coatings are prone to failure—such as suspension springs that experience high deflection and stone impact—the diffusion coating approach offers a corrosion protection mechanism that is metallurgically robust. The 50 μm thickness limit identified in the study provides a practical process window: thick enough to provide corrosion resistance, thin enough to be fully absorbed and to allow shot peening to remain effective.
