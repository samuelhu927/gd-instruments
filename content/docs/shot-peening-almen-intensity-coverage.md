---
title: "Shot Peening Intensity and Coverage: The Almen Strip as a Process Control Tool"
date: 2019-10-30
layout: "single"
---
Shot peening is a cold-working process in which small spherical media are propelled against the spring surface at high velocity, inducing a layer of compressive residual stress. The compressive layer opposes the tensile stresses that drive fatigue crack initiation and can extend fatigue life by a factor of three to five in high-strength steel springs. The effectiveness of shot peening is controlled by two primary parameters: intensity and coverage.

Intensity is a measure of the energy of the peening stream, and it is quantified using the Almen strip. An Almen strip is a thin strip of spring steel that is peened on one side under the same conditions as the spring. The compressive stresses induced by peening cause the strip to curve, and the arc height of the curvature is measured. The arc height, expressed in thousandths of an inch (or in millimeters), is the Almen intensity. A higher arc height indicates a more intense peening treatment, which generally corresponds to a deeper and higher-magnitude compressive residual stress layer. The relationship between Almen intensity and the actual residual stress distribution in the spring depends on the spring material, the media, and the peening parameters, but the Almen strip provides a reproducible process control metric.

Coverage is the percentage of the surface area that has been impacted by peening media. At 100% coverage, the entire surface has been hit at least once. Coverage is typically assessed visually by comparing the peened surface to a set of reference photographs, or by using a fluorescent tracer method. Coverage is not a linear function of peening time: the first 50% coverage is achieved relatively quickly, but the time required to go from 98% to 100% coverage is disproportionately long. In practice, 100% coverage is often specified for fatigue-critical springs, and the peening time is set to achieve this coverage with some margin.

The interaction between intensity and coverage determines the residual stress profile. A high-intensity peening treatment at low coverage may produce a deep compressive layer but leave areas of the surface unprotected. A low-intensity treatment at high coverage produces a uniform but shallow compressive layer. For optimal fatigue resistance, the peening parameters must be selected to produce a compressive layer that extends beyond the depth of the maximum applied tensile stress and that covers the entire surface uniformly. This requires both adequate intensity and full coverage.

The Almen strip is a control tool, not a direct measurement of the spring’s residual stress. The strip and the spring may respond differently to the same peening stream because of differences in geometry, material, and fixturing. For critical applications, the residual stress distribution in the spring itself can be measured by X-ray diffraction or by incremental hole drilling, and these measurements can be used to calibrate the Almen intensity to the actual spring performance. The Almen strip remains the day-to-day process control method because it is inexpensive, reproducible, and sensitive to changes in the peening equipment.
