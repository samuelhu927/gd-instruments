---
title: "Hydrogen Embrittlement After Electroplating: Mechanism, Detection and Prevention"
date: 2019-06-04
layout: "single"
---
Hydrogen embrittlement is a delayed fracture phenomenon that occurs when atomic hydrogen diffuses into high-strength steel and accumulates at microstructural traps such as grain boundaries, inclusions, and dislocations. The hydrogen reduces the cohesive strength of the lattice, allowing cracks to initiate and propagate at stress levels far below the material’s normal yield strength. In electroplated springs, the hydrogen is introduced during the plating process—particularly during acid cleaning, cathodic electrocleaning, and the plating itself—and the fracture may occur hours or days after plating, often during storage or in the first few cycles of service.

The metallurgical signature of hydrogen embrittlement is intergranular fracture. The crack follows the prior austenite grain boundaries, and the fracture surface shows a brittle, faceted appearance with little or no plastic deformation. This is in contrast to the transgranular cleavage or ductile dimple rupture that might be expected from an overload or fatigue failure. The intergranular morphology is a direct consequence of hydrogen segregation at grain boundaries, where it weakens the boundary and provides a preferential path for crack propagation.

Detection of hydrogen embrittlement is challenging because the failure is delayed and the hydrogen content may be below the detection limit of routine chemical analysis. The most reliable approach is a mechanical test: a sustained load test in which the plated spring is loaded to a stress below its yield strength and held for a specified time. If the spring fractures within the hold time, the plating process or the material is considered susceptible. The test conditions—stress level, hold time, and environment—must be specified, because a spring that survives a 24-hour hold at 75% of yield may still fail at a longer hold time or at a higher stress.

Prevention is more effective than detection. The primary control is to minimize hydrogen absorption during plating. Acid cleaning times should be minimized, cathodic electrocleaning should be avoided or carefully controlled, and the plating bath chemistry and current density should be optimized to maximize efficiency and minimize hydrogen evolution. For high-strength springs, a post-plating bake is mandatory: the spring is heated to a temperature in the range of 200–250°C for a period sufficient to allow hydrogen to diffuse out of the steel. The bake must be performed as soon as possible after plating, because hydrogen continues to diffuse and accumulate at traps even at room temperature, and a delay allows the hydrogen to reach a more stable—and more damaging—distribution.

The design of the spring also affects its susceptibility. Higher strength levels increase the sensitivity to hydrogen embrittlement, so a spring that is over-designed in strength may be more vulnerable than a spring with a lower strength and a larger cross-section. Surface defects—grinding marks, tool marks, corrosion pits—act as stress concentrators and hydrogen traps, so the surface finish of a plated spring is a critical variable. Shot peening before plating can introduce compressive residual stresses that oppose hydrogen-assisted crack initiation, but the peening must be performed before plating and the plating must not be so thick that it relaxes the compressive layer.
