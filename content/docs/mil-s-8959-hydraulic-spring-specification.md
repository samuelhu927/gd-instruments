---
title: "MIL-S-8959: The Legacy Military Specification for Hydraulic Springs"
date: 2019-11-05
layout: "single"
---
MIL-S-8959 is a United States military specification titled “Spring, Hydraulic: General Specification For,” covering the general requirements for hydraulic springs of class 1 and 2. Although the specification is inactive for new design and is no longer used except for replacement purposes, it remains instructive because it illustrates how military spring specifications translate functional requirements into acceptance tests that differ from commercial spring standards.

The core of MIL-S-8959 is a cycling test that simulates the hydraulic spring’s service duty. The specification requires cycling the unit 1,000 full design strokes from zero load to 100 percent applied load, with the maximum compressed stroke not exceeding 95 percent of the full stroke. The cycling rate is specified as 1 inch per second unless otherwise dictated by design requirements. This test is not a fatigue test in the conventional sense—1,000 cycles is far below the high-cycle regime—but a verification that the spring and its associated hydraulic components can complete repeated full-stroke operation without leakage, permanent set, or mechanical interference. The 95 percent stroke limit is a deliberate margin that prevents the spring from reaching solid height during the test, because solid-height contact would introduce a different loading condition and potentially mask a design deficiency in the normal stroke range.

The specification also addresses class 1 and class 2 hydraulic springs, which differ in their design and application but share the requirement for predictable force-displacement behavior and structural integrity under repeated cycling. In the military context, the acceptance logic is driven by reliability and interchangeability rather than by optimizing for minimum weight or cost. A replacement hydraulic spring must match the original in form, fit, and function, and the cycling test provides a standardized method for demonstrating that the replacement will not introduce a new failure mode.

While MIL-S-8959 is no longer active for new designs, its structure remains relevant to modern spring engineering in two ways. First, it demonstrates that a spring specification can be written around a functional test—the cycling sequence—rather than around material properties alone. A spring that meets the dimensional and material requirements but fails the cycling test is not acceptable, because the cycling test captures the interaction between the spring, its guides, and the hydraulic medium that a material certificate cannot. Second, the specification’s emphasis on full-stroke cycling with a defined margin from solid height reflects a design philosophy that remains sound: the spring should be tested in the range in which it will actually operate, with a clear boundary between normal operation and an abnormal solid-height condition.

For manufacturers working with legacy military or aerospace platforms, MIL-S-8959 provides a reference for the cycling test conditions that may be invoked in a replacement part qualification. Even when the specification is not cited directly, the 1,000-cycle full-stroke test at 1 inch per second is a useful screening protocol for hydraulic spring designs where repeated actuation and load retention are the primary functional requirements.
