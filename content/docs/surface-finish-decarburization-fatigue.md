---
title: "The Role of Surface Finish and Decarburization in Spring Fatigue Life"
date: 2020-12-15
layout: "single"
---
Fatigue cracks in springs almost always initiate at the surface. The surface is where the applied stress is highest, where defects are most likely to be present, and where environmental attack first occurs. Two surface conditions—roughness and decarburization—have a disproportionate influence on fatigue life, and both must be controlled to achieve the fatigue performance that the spring design assumes.

Surface roughness acts as a stress concentrator. A machining mark, a grinding scratch, or a drawing defect creates a local stress that is higher than the nominal stress in the smooth wire. The fatigue notch factor for a sharp surface defect can be several times the nominal stress, which means that the fatigue limit of a rough spring can be significantly lower than that of a polished spring of the same material and geometry. The effect is most pronounced in high-strength spring steels, where the material is less able to accommodate local stress concentration by plastic deformation. For springs that are designed for high-cycle fatigue, the surface roughness specification is not a cosmetic requirement; it is a fatigue-life requirement.

Decarburization is the loss of carbon from the surface layer of steel during heating. It occurs when the steel is exposed to an atmosphere that is oxidizing or decarburizing—for example, air or combustion products—at elevated temperature. The decarburized layer consists of ferrite, which has lower hardness and lower strength than the underlying tempered martensite. This soft surface layer is not capable of carrying the same stress as the core, so the effective cross-section of the spring is reduced, and the surface that remains is weaker than the material specification implies. More importantly, the decarburized layer is where fatigue cracks initiate, because the soft ferrite deforms plastically at stresses that the core can carry elastically, and the incompatibility between the soft surface and the hard core creates a local stress concentration.

The depth of decarburization that is tolerable depends on the spring’s stress level and fatigue requirement. For a spring that operates at low stress and is not fatigue-critical, a small decarburized layer may be acceptable. For a high-stress, high-cycle spring, the decarburized layer must be removed entirely—by grinding, by machining, or by choosing a wire that has been processed to avoid decarburization. The specification of “no decarburization” on a spring drawing means that the surface carbon content must be at the specified level, not merely that the visible oxide scale has been removed.

Surface finish and decarburization interact with shot peening. Shot peening can improve the fatigue resistance of a decarburized surface by introducing compressive residual stresses, but it cannot restore the lost strength of the decarburized layer. If the decarburized layer is deep and the shot peening is shallow, the compressive layer may be confined to the weak ferrite, and the benefit will be limited. The correct sequence is to remove the decarburized layer first—by grinding or machining—and then shot peen the fresh surface. This is why the manufacturing sequence for critical springs often includes a grinding step after heat treatment and before shot peening, and why the grinding depth is specified to ensure that the decarburized layer is completely removed.
