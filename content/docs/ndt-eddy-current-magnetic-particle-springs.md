---
title: "Nondestructive Testing of Springs: Eddy Current vs Magnetic Particle Inspection"
date: 2019-03-19
layout: "single"
---
Nondestructive testing is a critical quality control tool in spring manufacturing, and two methods dominate the inspection of surface and near-surface defects: eddy current testing and magnetic particle inspection. The choice between them depends on the material, the defect type, and the production context.

Magnetic particle testing is the preferred surface method for ferromagnetic spring steels. The spring is magnetized, and fine magnetic particles are applied to the surface. Defects that interrupt the magnetic field—cracks, laps, seams—produce a leakage field that attracts the particles, making the defect visible. Magnetic particle testing has high sensitivity to transverse and longitudinal cracks, and the results are intuitive: the inspector sees the indication directly. The limitations are that it applies only to ferromagnetic materials, and small dot inclusions and pits are easily missed because they do not produce a strong leakage field. For carbon and low-alloy spring steels, magnetic particle testing is a standard inspection method, particularly for springs that have been formed, heat treated, and ground.

Eddy current testing uses an alternating current coil to induce eddy currents in the surface of the spring. A defect that interrupts the eddy current flow changes the impedance of the coil, and this change is detected and interpreted. Eddy current testing is applicable to both ferromagnetic and non-ferromagnetic materials, which makes it the method of choice for stainless steel and non-magnetic springs. It is also well suited to automated inline inspection: the coil can be positioned close to the spring surface, and the inspection can be performed at production speed. The limitation of eddy current testing is that it is primarily sensitive to surface and near-surface defects; deep internal defects are not reliably detected. Non-uniform permeability in ferromagnetic materials can also reduce the signal-to-noise ratio, requiring careful calibration and signal processing.

In practice, the two methods are complementary rather than competitive. Magnetic particle inspection is used for final inspection of ferromagnetic springs where the highest sensitivity to surface cracks is required. Eddy current testing is used for inline inspection of wire or finished springs where speed and automation are important, and for non-ferromagnetic springs where magnetic particle testing is not applicable. Some critical applications specify both methods: eddy current for a rapid scan of the entire surface, followed by magnetic particle inspection of suspect areas or of the entire part for confirmation.

The selection of the NDT method should be documented in the spring specification, along with the acceptance criteria and the calibration requirements. A spring that is inspected with the wrong method—for example, eddy current on a ferromagnetic spring where the defect is a subsurface inclusion—may pass inspection and still fail in service. The NDT method is not a generic quality stamp; it is a specific test for a specific defect type, and the choice must be justified by the failure modes that the inspection is intended to detect.
