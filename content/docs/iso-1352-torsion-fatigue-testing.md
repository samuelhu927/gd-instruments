---
title: "ISO 1352 and Torque-Controlled Fatigue Testing for Torsion Springs"
date: 2020-09-14
layout: "single"
---
ISO 1352 specifies the conditions for performing torsional, constant-amplitude, nominally elastic stress fatigue tests on metallic specimens without deliberately introducing stress concentrations. For torsion springs, this standard provides the testing framework that the more commonly cited axial fatigue standards do not cover, because the stress state in a torsion spring is dominated by shear rather than by normal stress.

The standard applies to specimens with a nominal diameter between 5 and 12.5 mm, tested at ambient or elevated temperature. The loading is torque-controlled, meaning that the test machine applies a cyclic torque of constant amplitude and the angular displacement is measured as a response. This is distinct from displacement-controlled testing, where the angle is prescribed and the torque is measured. The choice between torque control and angle control depends on the service condition: a torsion spring that operates against a defined torque (for example, a spring-loaded hinge that must exert a specified moment) is better represented by torque-controlled testing, while a spring that operates at a defined deflection is better represented by angle-controlled testing.

The standard emphasizes “nominally elastic” stress, meaning that the stress amplitudes are below the yield strength of the material. This restriction is important because torsion springs in service are designed to operate in the elastic range, and the fatigue behavior of the material in the elastic regime is different from the low-cycle fatigue behavior that occurs when significant plastic deformation is present. For torsion springs that are expected to undergo high-cycle fatigue, the torque amplitudes must be selected so that the maximum shear stress remains below the torsional yield strength.

The test results are presented as a torque-life curve, analogous to the stress-life curve for axial fatigue. The torque amplitude is plotted against the number of cycles to failure, and the fatigue limit is determined at the required cycle count. For torsion springs, the failure criterion is usually fracture or a specified loss of torque-carrying capacity. The angular displacement at failure can also be recorded to provide insight into the stiffness degradation of the spring.

A practical challenge in torsion spring fatigue testing is the design of the grips. The spring must be gripped at both ends in a way that transmits the torque without introducing axial or bending loads. If the grips are not aligned with the spring axis, the spring experiences a combined loading state that is not representative of pure torsion, and the fatigue data will be invalid. The grips must also accommodate the change in length that occurs as the spring winds and unwinds under torque. For these reasons, torsion fatigue testing is generally more demanding in fixturing than axial fatigue testing, and the test setup must be validated before the data can be considered reliable.
